Reducing exposure to indoor air pollution in developing countries
In a review of ways of reducing smoke levels, interventions were considered in three areas: the source of smoke, the living environment and the user.

The most effective means of reducing indoor air pollution is to switch from biomass* to cleaner fuel that produces significantly lower emissions. For those who are able to switch to fuels such as kerosene or Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG), the benefits are great. In many urban areas these fuels cost less per unit than biomass. However, there is often a larger initial investment needed to purchase the fuels and the stoves. For example, LPG must be purchased each week or month by the cylinder, but many people can only afford to get a little fuel daily. Making fuel available in smaller units would benefit poorer customers. In rural areas there is less incentive to switch fuels, as biomass is gathered at no financial cost to the user.

Another alternative fuel, biogas, is extremely effective, as it converts a renewable material (dung and other organic waste materials) into a gaseous, clean fuel. While biogas is being introduced in parts of Asia very successfully – there are over 120,000 biogas plants in Nepal alone – traditional attitudes in other regions make it harder to introduce.

• • •

For those trapped into using biomass as their main domestic fuel for the foreseeable future, options for reducing exposure to indoor air pollution will entail safe ways of getting smoke out of the home. Substantial reductions to smoke exposure have been obtained with relatively simple methods. For example, one project in Kenya reduced particulate and carbon monoxide pollution in homes by nearly 80% through the use of smoke hoods. These work on the same principle as chimneys but have the advantage of being freestanding and independent of the stove. Substantial benefits were also achieved by increasing the roof spaces in traditional houses and installing better-fitting doors. However, the enlargement of windows in the same project seemed to have little impact.

• • •

Improved stoves were primarily designed to increase energy efficiency. As many of them incorporate a chimney, one would anticipate that smoke would be reduced. There are some very effective chimney stoves which, when tested in the home, show a significant reduction in smoke. Good examples are the rocket stove and Ecostove, which are increasingly being used in Central America.

However, there are also potential problems with many chimney stoves: they may not perform well if they are not installed properly, or are poorly designed. Chimneys are expensive and may be ineffective if the smoke returns through doors and windows. They can also block up quickly with soot and require regular cleaning.

• • •

A very simple technology can reduce the need for fuel for cooking – this is the fireless cooker, or 'hay box'. This acts like a slow cooker, and is good for making soups, rice or stews. The food is heated to boiling, then placed in a box filled with insulating material, such as hay or crumpled newspaper. The food continues to cook slowly. The development organisation Winrock found that the hay box was in demand with women's groups in Nairobi, where they are proving as popular as improved stoves.
