What you will learn

The past is not one tense but a small team. Each form shows the same past from a different angle: a finished event, a scene in progress, an earlier event, a long activity, or a habit that has ended. Tap through the tabs, learn one angle at a time, and by the end you will tell stories in clear, correct order.

Look at the five forms on one time line. The blue marker is NOW; everything else is in the past.

FormPatternThe idea in plain words
Past simpleworked · went · did you…?a finished event, or a finished habit or state
Past continuouswas / were + -ingan action in progress at a past moment; the scene or background
used to / wouldused to + verba past habit or state that is no longer true
Past perfecthad + past participlesomething that happened earlier than another past moment
Past perfect continuoushad been + -inghow long something had been going on, or the activity that caused a result
📖
In plain wordsTelling a story in the past is like directing a film. The past simple is the main plot. The past continuous is the set and the background action. The past perfect is the flashback: something that had already happened before the scene began.
IELTS tipSpeaking Part 2 (“Describe a time when…”) and Writing Task 2 examples need correct past forms. Using the past perfect and past continuous correctly, not just the past simple, shows range and accuracy.

Part 1

Past simple

The tense for finished actions, finished habits and finished states.

🕰️
In plain wordsPast simple = “it started and finished in the past.” The time may be said (in 1998), known, or clear from the story.

Form

I / you / he / she / it / we / they+verb-ed·irregular form
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
all subjectsShe returned · He leftShe didn't return
(did not)		Did she return?Yes, she did.
No, she didn't.

After did / didn't the main verb goes back to its basic form: Did she return? (not Did she returned?). Was / were (the past of be) make their own questions and negatives: Was he late? — He wasn't late.

Regular verbs add -ed (return → returned). Irregular verbs change form (leave → left, win → won, bring → brought). You will find the full list of irregular verbs in Lesson 30.

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

An action at a definite time in the pastcore

Is the time said, known or clearly finished?
  • The company announced a merger last month. (the time is stated)
  • We visited Lisbon in 2019.
  • When did the talks collapse? (the time is known)
b

Actions that follow one anothercore

Does one action come after the other, like steps in a story?
  • First she paid the driver, then she got out of the taxi.
  • He opened the file, read the first page and called his lawyer.
c

Past habits and states that are now finishedcore

Was it true for a period, but it is not true now?
  • Kitchens were very different a hundred years ago.
  • My grandfather worked on the railways for forty years. (he has retired)
d

The lives of people who are no longer alivecore

Is the person dead? Then we use the past simple even without a date.
  • William Shakespeare wrote “Romeo and Juliet”.
  • Marie Curie won two Nobel Prizes.
e

Hopes and plans that did not happenless common

Do you say what you hoped or planned, but it did not happen?
  • I hoped to finish early, but the meeting lasted all afternoon. (hope, expect, think, mean, intend, want can also be in the past continuous or past perfect with no change of meaning)

Time words

yesterdaylast night / week / month / yearthree days agoin 1997thenwhenHow long ago…?the other dayat that timeonceat the weekend

Be careful with ago: it counts back from now (five years ago). To count back from a past moment, use before (She had left school six months before).

Typical mistakes

Did she returned?
Did she return?
I didn't went.
I didn't go.
Yesterday I have met my tutor.
Yesterday I met my tutor.
IELTS tipIn Speaking Part 2 you tell a past story. Use first, then, next, after that, later, finally to show the order, and keep the main events in the past simple.
Part 2

Past continuous

The tense for something in progress at a past moment, and for the scene behind the story.

🎬
In plain wordsPast continuous = “it was in progress, like a film paused at that moment.” You do not say when it started or finished.

Form

I / he / she / it+was+verb-ing·you / we / they+were+verb-ing
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
I / he / she / itHe was watchingHe wasn't watchingWas he watching?Yes, he was.
No, he wasn't.
you / we / theyThey were watchingThey weren't watchingWere they watching?Yes, they were.
No, they weren't.

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

An action in progress at a stated time in the pastcore

Can you picture the action “still happening” at that moment?
  • At seven o'clock yesterday they were having dinner. (we do not know when it began or ended)
  • What were you doing at ten last night?
b

A longer action interrupted by a shorter onecore

Was one action going on when another suddenly happened?
  • He was walking down the street when he ran into an old friend. (longer = past continuous, shorter = past simple)
  • The lights went out while we were watching the film.
c

Two actions happening at the same timecore

Are two actions parallel?
  • She was talking on her phone while she was driving to work.
  • While I was cooking, he was setting the table.
d

The scene and atmosphere at the start of a storycore

Is it the background, before the main events begin?
  • One autumn afternoon, Ben was strolling down a quiet country lane. The birds were singing and the leaves were rustling in the breeze.
e

A temporary, repeated annoyance in the past (with always)less common

Did something repeat, and it annoyed you?
  • My old flatmate was always leaving dirty plates in the sink.

Time words

whilewhenasall morning / evening / day / nightat that momentat 7 o'clock yesterdaythe whole time
🚫
State verbsVerbs of state (know, like, want, need, believe, belong, seem) do not take the continuous: She knew the answer (not was knowing). Compare Lesson 1.

Typical mistakes

I was walking home when I was seeing an accident.
I was walking home when I saw an accident.
At 8 p.m. we watched TV. (we were in the middle of it)
At 8 p.m. we were watching TV.
Part 3

Past simple or past continuous?

Same two tenses, different jobs. The simple moves the story forward; the continuous describes what was going on around it.

Same verb, two ideas:

When she got home, she cooked dinner.past simple + past simple: one action after the other
When she got home, her flatmate was cooking dinner.past simple + past continuous: dinner was already in progress

Quick decision

Ask yourselfAnswerUse
Did the action start and finish (a single event or step)?yespast simple
Was it in progress and you want to stress that?yespast continuous
Did a short event interrupt it?yescontinuous for the long action + simple for the short one
Are the actions steps in a story?yesall in the past simple

when, while and as

  • while + a longer action (usually past continuous): While the manager was speaking, a phone rang.
  • when + a short event, or a point in time: The alarm went off when we were having a meeting.
  • as = at the same time as: As the children were playing, it started to pour.

Put a comma after the time clause when it comes first.

Linking words for a sequence of events

firstthennextafter thatlaterfinallyin the endat lastmeanwhile

Use them with the past simple: First, they travelled to Brighton by train. Then they looked around the shops.

IELTS tipFor Speaking Part 2 build a mini-story with the three-step pattern: background (past continuous) → event (past simple) → earlier cause (past perfect).
Part 4

used to, would, be used to and get used to

Four expressions that look alike and mean very different things.

ExpressionPatternMeaningExample
used toused to + verba past habit or state that is no longer trueI used to play the violin.
wouldwould + verba repeated past action (not a state)We would eat out on Sundays.
be used tobe used to + noun / -ingfamiliar with something; it feels normalShe is used to the cold.
get used toget used to + noun / -ingbecoming familiar with somethingHe got used to working at night.

used to and would

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

used to + verb: past habits and statescore

Was it true in the past, but not now?
  • He used to go out a lot when he was younger. (he doesn't any more)
  • They used to live in London.
  • Did she use to spend a lot of money when she was single? (questions and negatives: did / didn't use to)
b

would for repeated actionscore

Was it a repeated action? Then would is possible. Is it a state? Then only used to.
  • We would (or used to) eat out on Sundays.
  • I used to have a pet dog. (NOT I would have a pet dog — have here is a state)
⛔
Do NOT use used to for…one action at a definite time (I drove to work yesterday), or a number of times (I went to the cinema four times last month). Use the past simple.

be used to and get used to

Both are followed by a noun, pronoun or -ing form. They show that something is (or is becoming) familiar.

  • be used to = be accustomed to: They are used to hot weather. I'm used to getting up early. She wasn't used to working at night.
  • get used to = become accustomed to: I am getting used to the weather. He didn't like computers at first, but he got used to them. She will soon get used to wearing contact lenses.
used to + VERBa past habit — it is over
be / get used to + -INGfamiliar / becoming familiar — it is happening now or soon

Typical mistakes

I used to go to the cinema twice last month.
I went to the cinema twice last month.
She is used to get up early.
She is used to getting up early.
Did you used to smoke?
Did you use to smoke?
Part 5

Past perfect

The flashback tense: something that had already happened before another past moment.

⏪
In plain wordsPast perfect = “this happened BEFORE that.” Two past events: the earlier one gets had + past participle.

Form

had+past participle
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
all subjectsShe had arrived / had goneShe hadn't arrived
(had not)		Had she arrived?Yes, she had.
No, she hadn't.

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

An action before another past action, or before a stated past timecore

Which happened first?
  • They had already reserved the table when they went to the restaurant. (reservation first, restaurant second)
  • They had already sat down for dinner by 8 p.m.
b

An action whose result was visible in the pastcore

Could you see the result at that past time?
  • Bill had injured his legs in a car accident, so he had to use a wheelchair for six months.
  • He was happy: he had signed an important contract.
c

The past equivalent of the present perfectcore

Does the story happen in the past instead of now?
  • He has broken his leg, so he can't walk. (present perfect — result now)
  • He had broken his leg, so he couldn't walk. (past perfect — result then)

Time words

beforeafteralreadyjustforsincetill / untilwhenbyby the timenever … beforeas soon asthe first time

When the order is already clear from before, after, as soon as, because or from the story, the past simple is also correct: After she had finished / finished work, she met her friends.

Past simple, past continuous or past perfect?

Compare three different sequences with the same event:

SentenceWhat it means
When Jim came home, they watched a film.Jim came home, then they watched a film together.
When Jim came home, Mary was watching a film.She was still watching it when he arrived.
When Jim came home, Mary had already watched the film.She finished it before he arrived.

Typical mistakes

When I arrived, the film already started.
When I arrived, the film had already started.
She said she has finished.
She said she had finished.
He had went home.
He had gone home.
Part 6

Past perfect continuous

The tense for “how long” before a past moment, and for the activity that explains a result you could see then.

⏳
In plain wordsPast perfect continuous = “it had been going on for a while before that moment.” Use it for duration (for / since) and for visible results.

Form

had+been+verb-ing
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
all subjectsHe had been cryingHe hadn't been cryingHad he been crying?Yes, he had.
No, he hadn't.

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

The duration of an action before another past action or timecore

Do you say how long, with for or since?
  • They had been looking for a house for six months before they found one they liked.
  • They had been skating together for five years before they entered the competition.
b

An action that lasted for a time and whose result was visible in the pastcore

Can you see the result of the activity at that moment?
  • She had been working hard all day, so she was tired.
  • He was annoyed. He had been waiting at the airport for three hours.
🔗
The past equivalentPast perfect continuous is to the past what present perfect continuous is to now. He has been waiting for hours, so he is bored. → He had been waiting for hours, so he was bored.

Simple or continuous? Finished result or activity?

Past perfectPast perfect continuous
a finished action, a number, a result: She had written ten pages.an activity and its length: She had been writing for hours.
how many / whathow long
can be used with state verbs: I had known him for years.never with state verbs
IELTS tipIn an essay you rarely need the past perfect continuous. In Speaking Part 2, it is a good “band 7+” detail: I had been living abroad for two years when I realised…

Typical mistakes

He was tired because he had been run.
He was tired because he had been running.
She had been knowing him for years.
She had known him for years.
Part 7

All the past forms together

Now mix them. Use the checklist and the story pattern, then practise with longer texts.

Story pattern

1 · Backgroundwas / were + -ing — the scene
2 · Main eventspast simple — step by step, with first / then / next
3 · Earlier eventspast perfect — the flashback
4 · Cause / lengthpast perfect continuous — how long, or why a result was visible

Time words and their forms

Time wordCommon formExample

Part 8

Mistakes and rewriting

Typical learner mistakes with past forms, and the “say it another way” exercises that appear in many exams.

Say it another way: useful patterns

PatternExample
It is five years since + past simpleIt is five years since they moved = They moved five years ago.
last + past simpleWe haven't been for a year = We last went a year ago.
The last time + subject + past simpleThe last time I visited was in March.
never + past perfect (… before)It was the first time she had given a presentation = She had never given a presentation before.
wait until … before …He didn't start until everyone had sat down = He waited until everyone had sat down before he started.
be on the point of + -ingI was about to book = I was on the point of booking.

Word power: prepositions and phrasal verbs

Past-tense stories often use fixed prepositions and phrasal verbs. Learn them as small chunks.

Verb + preposition

ExpressionExample
arrive in (a big place) / arrive at (a small place) / get toThe delegates arrived in Geneva. They arrived at the station. They got to the bank.
believe inI don't believe in ghosts.
concentrate onHe couldn't concentrate on his work.
discuss (no preposition) sth with sbWe discussed the plan with the manager.
famous for · afraid of · full ofSpain is famous for its festivals. I'm afraid of spiders.
take care of · care forLauren takes care of our children. I don't care for rock music.

Phrasal verbs with bring

Phrasal verbMeaningExample
