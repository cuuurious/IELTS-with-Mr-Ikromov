Lesson 3 · Tenses

What you will learn

English has no single future tense. Instead we choose a form according to what we want to say: a prediction, a plan, an arrangement, a decision made right now, an action in progress later, or something finished by a future deadline. Learn one angle per tab and you will choose quickly and correctly.

FormPatternThe idea in plain words
Future simplewill + verba prediction, a decision made now, a promise or offer
be going toam / is / are going to + verba plan, or a prediction from evidence
Present continuousam / is / are + -inga fixed arrangement in the near future
Present simpleleaves · startsa timetable or programme
Future continuouswill be + -ingin progress at a future time, or a routine / arrangement
Future perfectwill have + participlefinished before a future time
Future perfect continuouswill have been + -inghow long, up to a future time
🧭
In plain wordsAsk yourself first: Is it a plan I made earlier (going to)? A decision now (will)? A diary entry (present continuous)? A timetable (present simple)? Then, only if needed, add the “in progress” or “finished by” angle.
IELTS tipSpeaking Part 3 questions about the future (“How will cities change?”) invite will + opinion language (I believe, probably, I doubt whether). Task 2 conclusions often use will for predictions.

Part 1

Future simple: will

The tense of predictions, instant decisions, promises and offers.

🔮
In plain wordswill = “this is what I think or decide now.” It does not say that a plan already exists.

Form

subject+will+basic verb
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
all subjectsHe will call
(’ll)		He won't call
(will not)		Will he call?Yes, he will.
No, he won't.

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

Predictions based on opinioncore

Do you think / believe / expect it will happen?
  • His parents think he will become an artist one day.
  • Perhaps the new rules will reduce pollution. (words that go with will: think, believe, expect, be sure, be afraid, probably, perhaps, certainly)
  • In 2050 there will be colonies on Mars.
b

On-the-spot decisions and offerscore

Are you deciding or offering at this very moment?
  • It's your birthday, so I'll pay for lunch. (offer)
  • I'll take this one. (decision)
c

Promises, threats, warnings, requests and hopescore

Are you promising, warning or asking?
  • I promise I won't tell anyone. (promise)
  • Stop talking or I'll send you out. (threat)
  • Factories must stop polluting or we won't be able to breathe. (warning)
  • I hope pollution levels will drop soon. (hope)
  • Will you open the door, please? (request)
d

Things that will definitely happen and we cannot controlcore

Is it simply going to happen, whatever we do?
  • The temperature will reach 40°C tomorrow.
  • Winter will set in early this year.
e

Refusing: won'tless common

Does someone refuse to do something?
  • I've talked to Sue, but she won't listen. (= she refuses to listen)

Will, shall and won't

  • Will you…? = request: Will you open the door?
  • Shall I…? = offer: Shall I give you a hand?
  • Shall we…? = suggestion: Shall we wait until the rain stops?
  • What shall I…? = asking for instructions or suggestions.

Will in real conversation

SituationWhat you sayWhy will?
A friend has a heavy bag“Give it to me — I'll carry it.”an offer made at this moment
The phone rings while you are cooking“I'll get it!”a decision made on the spot
A child asks if you will be late“I won't be long, I promise.”a promise
Talking about 2050“I think robots will do most routine jobs.”an opinion about the future

Words that signal a prediction with will

I thinkI believeI expectI'm sureI'm afraidI doubt whetherprobablyperhapscertainlydefinitelyno doubt

In the exam, begin a prediction with one of these. It sounds natural and shows you know that the future is an opinion: I'm fairly sure public transport will become free in some cities.

Guessing about nowwill can also guess about the present: That will be the postman. They will have arrived by now. (More in the extra box.)

Typical mistakes

I will to call you.
I will call you.
He will comes tomorrow.
He will come tomorrow.
I think it doesn't will work.
I think it won't work.
Part 2

Be going to

For plans already made and for predictions you can see coming.

🎯
In plain wordsbe going to = “I have already decided” or “I can see it is about to happen.”

Form

am / is / are+going to+basic verb
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
I / he / she / it / we / you / theyI am going to stayShe isn't going to stayIs she going to stay?Yes, I am.
No, she isn't.

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

Plans, intentions and ambitionscore

Did you decide this before you speak?
  • I'm going to employ two more staff.
  • I'm going to become a famous violinist one day. (ambition)
  • Now that they have won the lottery, they are going to buy a big house.
b

Actions you have already decided to do sooncore

Have you made up your mind, but not necessarily fixed the time?
  • They are going to get married in three months. (they have decided it)
c

Predictions based on evidencecore

Can you see or know something that shows what will happen?
  • Look at the clouds! It is going to rain.
  • The acrobat is going to walk along the tightrope.

Two ideas: the plan and the evidence

Plan (already decided)Evidence (I can see it)
I'm going to apply for a scholarship.Look at the queue — we're going to be late.
She's going to study law.He's driving too fast. He's going to crash.
They're going to build a new library.The sky is black. It's going to pour.
will + opinionI think / I believe — no evidence needed
going to + evidence or planI can see it / I have decided

Two helpful notes

  • A common pattern: be going to for the plan, then will for details and comments. I'm going to have a party next week. I'll invite all my friends.
  • With verbs of movement (go, come), we prefer the present continuous: Sam is going to the market. Sheila is coming for tea.

Typical mistakes

I am going to travelling.
I am going to travel.
She going to leave.
She is going to leave.
Part 3

Will, going to, present continuous, present simple

These four can all talk about the future. The difference is the angle: decision, plan, arrangement or timetable.

Same verb, two ideas:

I'll meet you at six.will: a decision made now
I'm meeting Tom at six.present continuous: already arranged

Quick decision table

The speaker says…UseExample
“I decide now” / offer / promisewillI'll carry that for you.
“I decided before” (a plan)be going toI'm going to study medicine.
“It is in my diary” (person + time/place arranged)present continuousHe is leaving for Amsterdam in an hour.
“A timetable says so”present simpleThe film starts in ten minutes.
“I think / believe” (opinion)willI think prices will rise.
“I can see it coming” (evidence)be going toLook out! You're going to fall.

More ways to talk about the future

ExpressionMeaningExample
be about to + verbvery soon, almost nowThe lecture is about to begin.
be due to + verbexpected by a timetableThe train is due to arrive at six.
be likely to + verbprobablePrices are likely to rise.
be bound to + verbcertain; sure to happenHe is bound to pass.
be set to + verbready and expected toThe company is set to expand.
be on the point of + -ingat the very moment beforeShe was on the point of leaving.

These are not in the basic forms, but they make an IELTS answer sound more natural and give you grammar range.

IELTS tipIn Writing Task 1 projections (“The population will reach…”) use will; for official plans use is expected to / is projected to — but know the basic forms first.

The future in the past

Use these patterns for plans, hopes and intentions that did not happen, or that were still in the future at a past moment.

  • was / were going to · was to · was about to · was due to + verb: Mr Simon was going to resign, but the manager offered him a better salary. (he did not resign)
  • was on the point of + -ing: They were on the point of leaving when the phone rang. (they did not leave)

Formal: be to + infinitive

be to is formal and typical of news reports: The President is to visit Poland next Monday. Together with be sure to / be certain to / be bound to (certainty) and verbs such as decide, plan, intend, arrange, mean + to (plans: We intend to buy a bigger flat) it widens your range.

Part 4

After when, if, until… no will

One of the most common mistakes: using will after a time word.

⛔
The ruleAfter while, before, after, until / till, as, unless, when, whenever, if, once, as soon as, as long as, by the time, in case, on condition that, suppose we use the present simple (or present perfect) — even though the meaning is future.

Examples

WrongRight
I'll wait until you will finish.I'll wait until you finish / have finished.
Call me as soon as you will get back.Call me as soon as you get back.
If it will rain, we'll stay in.If it rains, we'll stay in.

But: when and if as question words

  • when as a question word / in a noun clause takes the future: When will they get married? I'm not sure when they will visit us. BUT time word: He'll call us when he gets there.
  • if (= whether) after I don't know, I wonder, I doubt takes the future: I doubt whether she will pass. BUT: If you call her, give her my regards.
Part 5

Future continuous

An action in progress at a future moment, or something that will happen anyway.

🎬
In plain wordsFuture continuous = “at that future time it will be in progress.”

Form

will+be+verb-ing
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
all subjectsHe will be workingHe won't be workingWill he be working?Yes, he will.
No, he won't.

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

An action in progress at a stated future timecore

Can you picture it happening at that time?
  • This time next week, I'll be skiing in Austria.
b

Something that will happen as a result of a routine or arrangementcore

Will it happen anyway, as part of your normal plans?
  • I'll be seeing them at the meeting anyway. (so I can pass on the message)
c

Polite questions about someone's planscore

Do you want to know if your request fits their plans?
  • Will you be going to the shops later? Could you get me a sandwich?
  • Will you be using the photocopier for long?

Typical mistakes

This time tomorrow I am lying on a beach.
This time tomorrow I will be lying on a beach.
Part 6

Future perfect and future perfect continuous

Finished by a future deadline, or how long up to a future time.

🏁
In plain wordsFuture perfect = “it will be done by then.” Future perfect continuous = “by then it will have been going on for…”

Form

will have+past participle·will have been+verb-ing
Future perfectFuture perfect continuous
affirmativeThey will have finishedI will have been working
negativeThey won't have finishedI won't have been working
questionWill they have finished?Will you have been working?

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

Future perfect: finished before a stated future timecore

Will it be complete by then?
  • They will have finished their meeting by four o'clock.
  • I will have tidied up by the time you get back.
  • She won't have completed the report until / till five o'clock. (until / till only in negative sentences — positive: by)
b

Future perfect continuous: the duration up to a future timecore

Do you stress how long, with by … for?
  • By the time Rick retires, he will have been working for the same company for thirty years.
  • By the time they reach York, they will have been travelling for four hours.
byby the timebeforeby thenuntil / till (negative)by … for

Note

After by the time, until, before we use the present simple because they introduce time clauses. The clause can come first or second: I will have tidied up by the time you get back. / By the time you get back, I will have tidied up.

Typical mistakes

I will have finished until Friday.
I will have finished by Friday.
By the time he will arrive, we will have left.
By the time he arrives, we will have left.
