Lesson 7 · Verb forms

What you will learn

Participles are verb forms that work as adjectives and let you build shorter, more elegant sentences. Too and enough both describe degree. This lesson covers all of them: the difference between -ing and -ed adjectives, three types of participle, participle clauses, and the rules for too and enough.

FormExamplesIdea
present participlestaying, leavingactive, “what something is like”
past participlestayed, leftpassive, “how someone feels”
perfect participlehaving leftfinished before the main action
✂️
In plain wordsA participle clause is a shortcut: it drops the subject and the small helping words. “Because she was tired, she went to bed” becomes “Being tired, she went to bed.”
IELTS tipParticiple clauses (Having analysed the data, the researchers concluded…) are a classic band 7+ feature in Writing Task 2 and Task 1. But the subject must be the same in both parts.

Part 1

-ing and -ed adjectives

The difference between boring and bored.

Same verb, two ideas:

It was an embarrassing situation.-ing: what kind of situation? (it causes the feeling)
He was embarrassed.-ed: how does he feel? (he has the feeling)

Rule

The present participle (-ing) describes what somebody or something is (it answers “What kind?”). The past participle (-ed) describes how somebody feels (it answers “How do you feel?”).

Part 2

Participle clauses

Shorter sentences with a participle.

Five uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

Instead of a relative pronoun and full verbcore

Can you drop who / which + is / are?
  • The woman waving at me is my aunt. (= who is waving)
  • The new shampoo advertised on TV is very expensive. (= which is advertised)
b

To express reasoncore

Does it mean because?
  • Feeling shy, Laura didn't talk to Ben.
  • Having seen the film before, I decided to stay at home. (= Because I had seen…)
  • Being late, Adam took a taxi.
c

To express timecore

Does it mean after / while?
  • After taking (or having taken) his Master's degree, he applied for a job.
  • We met John while shopping.
d

Instead of the past simple: actions in quick successioncore

Does one thing follow immediately after another in a story?
  • Hearing the news, she fainted. (= She heard the news and she fainted)
  • Seeing the shadow, he screamed.
e

To avoid repeating the past continuouscore

Are there two actions in progress together?
  • He was walking down the street whistling a tune.
  • She was climbing up a ladder carrying a bucket.

Which participle?

FormMeaningExample
-ing (present)active; same timeFeeling shy, she stayed quiet.
having + -ed (perfect)active; earlier actionHaving finished, he left.
-ed (past)passiveThe goods damaged in transport will be replaced.
not + participlenegativeNot knowing the answer, he guessed.
⚠️
One golden ruleThe subject of the participle is the subject of the main clause. Walking along the road, a bus hit him is wrong — the bus was not walking. Say: Walking along the road, he was hit by a bus.

Typical mistakes

Seeing the shadow, the scream was loud.
Seeing the shadow, he screamed.
Having finish, he left.
Having finished, he left.
Part 3

Too and enough

Too much, or just right?

too

Too comes before adjectives and adverbs. It shows that something is more than necessary or wanted, and it has a negative meaning.

PatternExample
too + adjective / adverb + to-infinitiveTim is too old to join the basketball team. = so old that he can't
too + adjective + for sb / sthThis plan is too complicated for me.
too + adjective + for sb + to-infinitiveThis ring is too expensive for me to buy.
too + adverbThey run too fast for me to catch up with them.

Do not repeat the object: too hot to drink (not to drink it).

enough

Enough comes after adjectives and adverbs but before nouns. It shows that there is as much as is needed: positive meaning.

PatternExample
adjective / adverb + enough + to-infinitiveHe is clever enough to solve the problem.
enough + noun + to-infinitiveWe have enough money to go on holiday.
(not) + adjective + enough + to-infinitiveWe are old enough to vote. We are not old enough to vote.

Same verb, two ideas:

The car is too expensive for me to buy.too: more than I can pay
The car is not cheap enough for me to buy.not enough: same meaning, with the opposite adjective

Typical mistakes

I am enough strong.
I am strong enough.
It is too hot to eat it.
It is too hot to eat.
She is not so talented enough.
She is not talented enough.
The tea is too much hot.
The tea is too hot.
IELTS tipIn Writing Task 2 “too” is often used for problems: Houses are too expensive for young people to buy. “Enough” gives solutions: There are not enough nurses to meet demand.
