Part 1

Present simple

The tense for things that are always, usually or generally true.

🕰️
In plain wordsPresent simple = “this is just how things are.” What you do regularly, what is always true, what a timetable says.

Same verb, two ideas:

He works in a bank.present simple → permanent: just how things are
He's working on a project this week.present continuous → temporary: in progress

Form

I / you / we / they+verb·he / she / it+verb-s
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
I / you / we / theyI workI don't work
(do not)		Do you work?Yes, I do.
No, I don't.
he / she / itHe worksHe doesn't work
(does not)		Does he work?Yes, he does.
No, he doesn't.

Remember: after doesn't / does the main verb goes back to its basic form — He doesn't work (not doesn't works).

Uses

core learn these first · less common learn these second

a

Permanent situations, habits and daily routinescore

Is it a permanent situation, or something that happens again and again?
  • My grandmother lives in a small village. (permanent situation)
  • Karim catches the 7:15 bus every morning. (routine / repeated action)
b

General truths and laws of naturecore

Is it always true — for everyone, everywhere?
  • Honey never goes bad.
  • Metal expands when it gets hot.
c

Timetables and programmescore (trains, flights, films, events)

Is a timetable or a programme deciding the time?
  • The concert starts at 8 p.m.
  • Our flight lands at 6:40.
d

Sports commentary, reviews and storytellingless common

Am I telling a story, a match or a review as if it is happening in front of me?
  • Ruiz passes to Ali, and Ali shoots! (commentary)
  • In this novel, the hero loses his memory. (review)
  • Then the stranger opens the door and walks in. (narration)
e

Instructions and directionsless common — we can say you + present simple instead of the imperative

Am I telling someone how to do something, step by step?
  • You take the second left, and then you walk to the end of the road. (= Take the second left…)

Typical mistakes here

Exam tip · IELTS Speaking Part 1Talk about your habits, routine and likes with the present simple and adverbs of frequency: I usually…, I rarely…, I enjoy…

Adverbs of frequency: how often?

They answer the question How often…?

always100%
usually≈ 75%
often≈ 50%
sometimes≈ 25%
rarely / seldom≈ 10%
never0%

Where do they go?

Emilyneverwatcheshorror films← before the main verb
Kimissometimesrude← after to be
Youmustalwaysbehave← after the first auxiliary / modal
1

Before the main verb, but after to be and after the first auxiliary or modal verb (do, have, can, must…).

  • Emily never watches horror films.
  • Kim is sometimes rude to people.
  • You must always behave yourself at school.
  • Does Roger often call you during the week?
2

In short answers the adverb goes before the auxiliary.

  • “Do you buy expensive clothes?” — “No, I never do.” / “Yes, I usually do.”
3

Never, rarely and seldom already have a negative meaning, so never use them with not or don't.

  • I rarely go to bed late. (not: I don't rarely…)
4

Frequency phrases (every day, once a week, twice a month, most mornings, most evenings…) go at the end or the beginning of the sentence.

  • We go on holiday twice a year.
  • Most evenings I walk by the river.
5

Usually, often, sometimes, normally, occasionally can also start the sentence or come at the end, for more emphasis.

  • Usually, I finish work at five.
  • I feel bored sometimes.
Watch outWith always in the present continuous the meaning changes: it shows annoyance (You're always losing your keys!) — see Part 2.

Typical mistakes with adverbs

She still is abroad.
She is still abroad.
Part 2

Present continuous

The tense for actions in progress, temporary situations and change.

🎥
In plain wordsPresent continuous = “it is in progress.” Like a video camera: now, around now, changing — or already arranged for the near future.

Form: am / is / are + verb-ing

am / is / are+verb-ing
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
II'm workingI'm not workingAm I working?Yes, I am. / No, I'm not.
he / she / itHe's workingHe isn't workingIs he working?Yes, he is. / No, he isn't.
you / we / theyThey're workingThey aren't workingAre they working?Yes, they are. / No, they aren't.

Uses

a

Actions happening nowcore, at the moment of speaking

Can I see or hear it happening at this moment?
  • Look — it 's snowing!
  • He 's giving the baby a bath at the moment.
b

Temporary situationscore — happening around now, but not necessarily at this exact second

Is it only for a short time, around now (not necessarily this second)?
  • I 'm reading a novel about pirates at the moment. (I'm not reading it this second.)
  • My brother is living with us until June.
c

Always / constantly / continually + continuousless common — to complain about something that happens too often, or just to say it happens very often

Does it happen too often — does it annoy me (or just happen very often)?
  • You 're constantly interrupting me when I'm talking! (annoyance)
  • I 'm always meeting Sara when I go shopping. (very often)
d

Fixed arrangements in the near futurecore — especially when the time and place are decided

Have the time and place already been decided?
  • We 're moving into our new house next week.
  • Melanie is getting married at three this afternoon.
e

Changing and developing situationscore

Is something getting bigger, smaller, better or worse?
  • More and more species are becoming extinct.
  • The climate is getting warmer every year.

Typical time expressions

nowat the momentat presentthese daysnowadaystodaytonightstill

Typical mistakes here

Joe plays in the garden at the moment.
Joe is playing in the garden at the moment.
Exam tip · IELTS Writing Task 2 and SpeakingFor changing situations use the present continuous: More and more species are becoming extinct. The climate is getting warmer.
Part 3

Present simple or present continuous?

Ask yourself: is it a permanent habit or fact — or is it happening now, temporary, changing or arranged?

Present simple
  • He works in a bank. (permanent)
  • It rains a lot in April. (general)
  • The bus leaves at nine. (timetable)
Present continuous
  • He 's working on a project this week. (temporary)
  • It 's raining now. (in progress)
  • The bus is leaving — run! (happening / arranged)
Part 4

State verbs

State verbs describe a state (what something is, feels, thinks or has), not an action. That is why they normally do not take continuous tenses: I know the answer — never I am knowing.

🧠
Memory trickState verbs are things you ARE, HAVE, FEEL or THINK — not things you DO. (I know the answer — you cannot “be knowing”.)

The main groups

Likes and dislikes

likelovehatedislikeenjoypreferadore

Thinking and knowing (perception)

knowbelieveunderstandrealiserememberforgetnoticerecognisethinkseemexpect (= think)see (= understand)

The senses

seehearsmelltastefeellooksound

For what you see or hear right now we use can / could: I can hear footsteps in the attic.

Other state verbs

becontainincludebelongfitneedmattercostmeanownowewantwishrequireweighkeep (= continue)have (= possess)

Verbs with two meanings

Many state verbs also have an action meaning — then the continuous is possible.

VerbState meaning (simple)Action meaning (continuous)
thinkI think she's Italian. (believe)I'm thinking about my holiday. (considering)
tasteThe soup tastes awful. (has a flavour)She 's tasting the soup. (testing the flavour)
seeI can see a plane. / I see what you mean. (perceive; understand)I'm seeing Jill tonight. (meeting)
lookSusan looks tired. (seems)Susan is looking at some photos. (directing her eyes)
smellThe room smells of perfume. (has a smell)The cat is smelling its food. (sniffing)
feelThis towel feels soft. (has a texture)Jill is feeling her son's forehead. (touching)
haveHe has a sports car. (possesses)He 's having lunch now. (eating — idiom)
weighThe chicken weighs two kilos. (has a weight)The butcher is weighing the meat. (measuring)
fitThis dress fits you perfectly. (is the right size)We are fitting new locks. (installing)
appearHe appears to be nervous. (seems)He is appearing in a new play. (performing)
beHe is selfish. (character — permanent)He is being selfish today. (behaviour — temporary; with adjectives like silly, rude, lazy, careful, polite)
Idioms with have (action — continuous is fine) have breakfast / lunch / dinner · have a bath / shower / swim / party · have an accident / experience / dream · have a baby · have difficulty / fun / trouble
Special cases

enjoy — simple for a general liking (I enjoy parties), continuous for pleasure right now (I'm enjoying this party a lot).

look (appearance), feel (emotion), hurt and ache can be used in either tense with no difference: You look / are looking great today. My back hurts / is hurting.

Part 5

Present perfect

The present perfect connects the past with the present. It describes actions that began in the past and continue up to now, or finished actions that matter now.

🌉
In plain wordsPresent perfect = a bridge: one end in the past, the other end in NOW. The past matters now.

Same verb, two ideas:

She has taken fifteen photos today.present perfect → today isn't over, she may take more
She took twenty photos yesterday.past simple → yesterday is finished (Lesson 2)

Form: have / has + past participle

have / has+past participle
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
I / you / we / theyI have ('ve) leftI haven't leftHave you left?Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.
he / she / itHe has ('s) leftHe hasn't leftHas he left?Yes, he has. / No, he hasn't.

Uses

a

A situation that began in the past and is still true nowcore — especially with state verbs (be, have, like, know…), usually with for or since

Did it start in the past and is it still true now?
  • Rachel has had the dog for three years. (She still has it.)
  • They have been friends for twenty years.
b

A recent action whose result is visible nowcore

Can I see the result right now?
  • She has just washed her hair. (It's wrapped in a towel.)
  • She has picked a lot of apples. (They're in the basket — the action is finished.)
c

An action at an unstated time in the pastcore — the exact time is unknown or unimportant; what matters is the fact (an experience, a number of times)

Is the exact time unknown or unimportant — only the fact matters?
  • The Taylors have bought a sailing boat. (Now they own one.)
  • Peter has been to Paris four times.
  • He has broken his arm.
d

An action within a time period that is not finished yetcore — today, this morning / week / month / year…

Is the time period (today, this week…) still going?
  • She has taken fifteen photos today. (Today isn't over — she may take more.)
  • She took twenty photos yesterday. (Yesterday is finished → past simple.)
  • She has received three faxes this morning. / She received three faxes this morning. (said in the afternoon — the morning is over)
Announcing newsWe use the present perfect to announce a piece of news, then the past simple (or past continuous) for the details: The police have finally arrested Peter Duncan. He was trying to leave the country when they caught him.

Typical mistakes here

I'm playing chess since I was eight.
I've been playing chess since I was eight.
The builders finish the block already.
The builders have already finished the block.
He has been breaking his arm.
He has broken his arm.
Sam have just finished reading.
Sam has just finished reading.
Who has use my scissors?
Who has used my scissors?
He hasn't still bought a computer.
He still hasn't bought a computer.
They still have called us.
They still haven't called us.
They haven't reached yet a decision.
They haven't reached a decision yet.
Exam tip · IELTS SpeakingUse the present perfect for experiences and unfinished situations: I've been to…, I've never…, I've lived here for…

Time expressions with the present perfect

WordMeaning and positionExample
fora length of timeI have known Jack for five years.
sincea starting point (a date, a moment, or another past event)They've been married since last April.
how longasks about durationHow long have you known Jack?
alreadysooner than expected — before the participle, or at the endWe have already seen this film. / Have you finished already?
yetuntil now — in questions and negatives, at the endHas Roger left yet? · Simon hasn't finished yet.
justa moment ago — between have and the participleI have just phoned Jill.
stillin negatives — shows surprise it hasn't happened; before haven't / hasn'tMy sister still hasn't called me back.
alwaysall through your life up to nowShe has always loved animals.
everat any time in your life — in questionsHave you ever been abroad?
neverat no time — negative meaning, so no notShe has never been to France.
so farup to now, counting what you've doneI have sent twenty invitations so far.

today, this week / month…a period that isn't overI've sent five emails this morning.
for = lengthfor + how long (a length of time)
since = startsince + when it started (a starting point)
for or since?for + how long (for two hours, for ages, for a long time)  ·  since + when it started (since 9 o'clock, since Monday, since 2015, since I was a child).

Have been to … or have gone to …?

been = backwent and came back
gone = awaywent and is still there
has gone to

The person went and is still there (or on the way).

  • “Where's Ana?” “She 's gone to the bank.” (She isn't back yet.)
has been to

The person went and has come back — it's an experience.

  • “Is Ana around?” “She 's been to the bank, so she's back.”
  • He has been to Japan twice.
Part 6

Present perfect continuous

The same link between past and present — but the focus is on the activity and how long it has lasted.

🚶
In plain wordsSame bridge as the present perfect — but now you look at the walking, not the arrival: the activity and how long it has lasted.

Form: have / has been + verb-ing

have / has+been+verb-ing
AffirmativeNegativeQuestionShort answers
I / you / we / theyI have ('ve) been readingI haven't been readingHave you been reading?Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.
he / she / itHe has ('s) been readingHe hasn't been readingHas he been reading?Yes, he has. / No, he hasn't.

Uses

a

The duration of an action that started in the past and continues up to nowcore — with for, since, how long, all morning / day / week

Do I want to say how long it has been going on?
  • Sam has been talking on the phone for half an hour. (He started half an hour ago and is still talking.)
  • How long have you been learning English?
b

An activity that has just stopped but whose result you can seecore (it may have lasted some time)

Has the activity just stopped — and can I see the result?
  • He's dirty. He 's been playing football. (He isn't playing now, but his clothes are muddy.)
  • Her feet hurt. She 's been walking all morning.
c

Irritation, anger or annoyanceless common

Am I irritated or annoyed?
  • Who has been reading my business papers? (The speaker is annoyed.)
  • Somebody has been giving away my secrets!
d

Time expressions: for · since · how long · lately · recently · all morning / day / week

Present perfect or present perfect continuous?

Present perfect → result / number
  • I 've typed four reports so far. (how many)
  • She has picked a lot of apples. (finished — the basket is full)
Perfect continuous → duration / activity
  • I 've been typing reports all morning. (how long)
  • She has been picking apples for two hours. (still doing it)
Three rules 1) With how many / how much / a number use the present perfect.   2) With how long and a continuing activity use the continuous.   3) State verbs (know, like, be, have…) cannot be continuous: I've known him for years — not I've been knowing.
No differenceWith live, work, teach and feel (an emotion) you can use either form with the same meaning: We have lived / have been living here for twenty years. He has felt / has been feeling unwell all morning.
Exam tip · IELTS SpeakingTo say how long, use the present perfect continuous: I've been learning English for…, I've been working here since…
Part 7

All four present forms together

TenseMain ideaSignal wordsExample
Present simplehabits, facts, permanent states, timetablesalways, usually, every day, on MondaysLena teaches chemistry.
Present continuousnow, temporary, changing, arranged futurenow, at the moment, these days, nowadays, stillLena is teaching a class now.
Present perfectpast + now: experience, result, unfinished timefor, since, already, yet, just, ever, never, so far, todayLena has taught here for ten years.
Present perfect continuousduration up to now, activity with a visible resultfor, since, how long, lately, all dayLena has been marking tests all evening.
Three quick questions1) Is it a habit or a fact? → present simple.   2) Is it happening now / around now or arranged? → present continuous.   3) Does it connect the past to now? → present perfect — and if you stress how long or the activity itself, use the continuous.
